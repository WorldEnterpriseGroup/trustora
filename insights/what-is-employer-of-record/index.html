EoR 101

What is an Employer of Record? A clear guide for global teams

The acronym is short. The decision is not. Here is the plain-language version of an EoR model—and the boundaries that make it useful.

Aug 11, 20267 min readBy Trustora

On this page

  1. The short version
  2. Why companies consider an EoR
  3. What the company keeps
  4. What an EoR usually coordinates
  5. What to ask before choosing a partner
  6. The honest conclusion

An Employer of Record is a local employment model for a company that wants to hire someone in a country where it does not yet have its own employing entity.

That sentence is simple on purpose. It keeps the useful idea in view: an EoR is an operating layer around employment, not a shortcut around responsibility.

The short version

The EoR is named as the local employer in the employment arrangement. It coordinates the employment administration that has to work in that country: the contract, payroll, statutory contributions, benefits, leave, onboarding, and offboarding. Your company still directs the day-to-day work, sets priorities, manages performance, and builds the relationship with the employee.

The exact division depends on the country, the agreement, and the provider. A good partner makes that division explicit before anyone signs anything.

Why companies consider an EoR

The most common reason is timing. A company has found the person it wants to hire, but opening a local entity would add cost, lead time, and a permanent commitment before the team knows whether the market, role, or operating model will settle.

An EoR can create a more reversible path: hire compliantly while learning, then decide later whether a local entity or another model makes sense. That does not make the decision consequence-free. It makes the decision more staged.

An EoR can also help when a team is distributed across several countries and wants one operating partner for the employment administration around each local relationship.

What the company keeps

An EoR does not replace the company’s role as the manager or the team builder. Your company usually keeps responsibility for:

  • deciding why the role exists and what the person will do;
  • recruiting, selecting, and managing the employee;
  • setting objectives, feedback, and day-to-day direction;
  • choosing the compensation philosophy and the working tools;
  • making sure the working relationship matches the facts.

That last point matters. A contract label, invoice, or company registration is not a complete answer to whether someone is an employee or an independent contractor. The applicable law and the real working relationship matter.

What an EoR usually coordinates

An EoR service commonly covers the employment administration around the person, such as:

  • local employment agreement coordination;
  • payroll processing and statutory contribution administration;
  • benefits and leave administration;
  • onboarding and required documentation;
  • employee question routing and changes during employment;
  • offboarding and final-pay coordination.

“Usually” is doing important work here. Do not compare EoR providers only by a list of nouns. Ask who owns each step, when approvals happen, what the employee sees, and where a country-specific question goes when the answer is not in the handbook.

What to ask before choosing a partner

Start with five questions:

  1. Who is the legal employer in the target country?
  2. What does the company still own, and what does the EoR coordinate?
  3. How are payroll, benefits, leave, and employee questions handled?
  4. Which work is performed by local specialists, and where does the provider’s role end?
  5. What happens if the employee, country, role, or business plan changes?

The best answer will not be the one with the most confident wording. It will be the one that gives you a usable map.

The honest conclusion

An EoR can make global hiring more manageable by creating a local employment layer without requiring an immediate entity build. It can help a company move with more context and less administrative drag.

It cannot remove the need for good worker classification, local advice, clear management, or a respectful employee experience. The point is not to make cross-border employment invisible. The point is to make it understandable enough to operate well.
