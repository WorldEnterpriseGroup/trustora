International onboarding is often described as a checklist. The employee experiences it as a test of whether the company is ready for the relationship it offered.

The first ten days should join the administrative and human work. The contract, payroll, benefits, equipment, access, manager context, and first contribution are not separate if the employee is the person carrying all of them.

## Before day one

Confirm the employing model, country, contract, pay date, benefits, leave, identity and right-to-work process where applicable, equipment, access owner, manager, working location, and support route. Make the unknowns visible instead of promising a smoothness no one has planned.

## Days one to three

The employee should meet the manager, understand the work, know who employs them, receive the first practical instructions, and have a way to ask an employment question without routing everything through the technical manager.

The manager should know the same basics. A brilliant role brief does not help if the manager cannot answer when the first pay statement arrives or who can help with a local benefits question.

## Days four to ten

Move from completion to contribution. Confirm access, working environment, team rituals, documentation, role expectations, and the first meaningful piece of work. Ask what is unclear. Small uncertainty is cheaper to resolve before it becomes a trust problem.

## The boundary around local advice

Cross-border social security, employment, tax, immigration, health and safety, and working-time questions depend on the facts and the employee’s location. European coordination rules, for example, can depend on where work is actually performed and how much work occurs in each country. An onboarding framework helps surface those questions; it does not replace qualified local advice.

Our operating test is simple: at the end of day ten, can the employee explain the relationship, the work, the support path, and what happens next?
